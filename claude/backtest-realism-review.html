Before you read

What this is based on, and how far to trust the numbers in it.

  • Scope. The engine as built at commit 37ad348 and the workbook layout in your five screenshots: Liquid, FX, Flows, Commitments, Spec.
  • Nothing here is measured on your data. Your workbook isn't on this machine, so every magnitude below is arithmetic under an assumption stated next to it. Two statements were checked against the engine on the sample workbook: the identity in 1, exact to 1e‑10 in USD and EUR, and the carry-forward behaviour in the next section.
  • “Bias today” in the summary says which way the current simplification leans. Flatters means the backtest looks better than an investor's experience would have been.

First, confirm one setting

Not a realism point, but it moves the result more than anything below it.

AnnualRatePolicy has carry_forward=False by default, and examples/eur_moderate.py runs with that default: a year's rate is used only if a fund of that type closes in that year, and is dropped otherwise. PEM closes every December, so BUYOUT is unaffected. Secondaries funds close every three or four years — so of the sixteen annual SECONDARIES rates for 2010–2025, five land on a closing year and eleven are never used.

Each rate applies to the liquid balance on its own closing date, so the sums show scale rather than dollars: as configured, the secondaries programme is about a third of the size the schedule implies. (SEC_X counts only if your Liquid series runs past December 2025; a fund closing after the last observation is never committed.) The engine confirms the mechanism on the sample workbook — SEC_VI is committed at 0.8% by default and 1.6% with carry-forward.

If the schedule is an annual budget — the plan in chatgpt/simulation-implementation-plan.html lists percentage carry-forward as agreed behaviour — switch it on:

load_profile_workbook(path, "EUR", "Moderate", initial_value, carry_forward=True)
# or, in examples/eur_moderate.py
spec = repository.simulation_spec(initial_value, carry_forward=True)

Two consequences. Pooled secondaries commitments become lumpy — 3% to 4.5% of the liquid balance sized on a single December month end — which is one reason to size on a trailing average (7). And no BUYOUT fund closes in 2024 or 2025 in the Spec sheet, so those years' BUYOUT rates wait for a PEM2025 row either way.

The suggestions

Each in the same four parts: what the engine does now, why that isn't what an investor experienced, what to change, and where.

1

Report the counterfactual and the PME — the engine already computes it

BenchmarkEffect: highEffort: SImplemented

Now

The result gives balances and flows and nothing to set them against. “Total value reached X” is not an answer until it stands next to the same liquid portfolio with no private programme.

Gap

Every call is paid by selling the liquid portfolio and every distribution buys it back. That makes the simulation a public-market-equivalent calculation against the investor's own portfolio — the most relevant benchmark there is — and the relationship is exact:

value_added(T) = total_close(T) − initial_value × I(T)/I(0)
               = Σ (distributions(t) − calls(t)) × I(T)/I(t)  +  private_close(T)

KS-PME         = ( Σ distributions(t) × I(T)/I(t) + private_close(T) )  /  Σ calls(t) × I(T)/I(t)

value_added(T) = FV(calls) × (KS-PME − 1)

I is the profile's liquid level; flows are the base-currency columns of result.periods. Checked against the engine on the sample workbook, in USD and in EUR with conversion: equal to 1e‑10.

Change

Report, for every run: the liquid-only path beside total_close; value added in currency and as a share of the counterfactual; KS-PME and Direct Alpha (the annualised form — the IRR of the flows once each is compounded to T by the index) for the programme, by fund type and by fund. Value added includes unrealised NAV, so read it with 10.

Built

pmsim/benchmark.py, reached through SimulationResult.compare_with_liquid_only() and SimulationResult.public_market_equivalent() — programme, each fund type, each fund; KS-PME, IRR and direct alpha. No new data: periods.return_factor compounds to the index, and nothing in the loop changed. examples/eur_moderate.py prints both tables and writes them as CSV. The tests assert the identity above on every benchmarked run, including through a shortfall.

2

Put both sides on the same fee basis

BenchmarkEffect: high if grossEffort: S

Now

The Flows sheet holds what an investor actually paid and received, so it is net of management fees, carry and fund expenses. The Liquid sheet is whatever the index is; a model-portfolio total return is usually gross of advisory fee, product costs and trading.

Gap

A gross liquid index overstates the opportunity cost of every call, the reinvestment return on every distribution, and the counterfactual itself. The bias runs against the private programme and it compounds: a 1% a year difference in all-in cost is about 15% of terminal liquid wealth over fifteen years.

Change

Establish what the Liquid returns are net of. If gross, apply the client's all-in cost as a monthly drag while compounding, and check whether a client would pay any feeder or programme-level fee on top of the flows in the sheet.

Where

pmsim/data/orchestrator.py: one parameter on returns_to_levels, surfaced on SimulationSpec.

3

Compare at equal risk

BenchmarkEffect: highEffort: S

Now

The natural counterfactual in 1 is the same profile with no programme, and calls are funded pro rata from the whole liquid portfolio.

Gap

Adding private equity to a Conservative portfolio raises its risk, by more than smoothed marks reveal (9). Part of the measured value added is payment for that risk, not a benefit of the programme. In practice an adviser adding a 10–15% equity-like sleeve often trims listed equity to keep the client in profile; here total risk drifts up as the private share grows.

Change

Report a second, risk-matched counterfactual: a blend of the profile and the next one up — the Liquid sheet has Conservative, Moderate and Aggressive for each currency — weighted so its volatility equals the unsmoothed volatility of the portfolio with the programme. If house practice is to fund privates from the equity sleeve, model that instead; it needs each profile's equity and bond components as separate return columns.

Where

Reporting only: a second Liquid series run through the same counterfactual calculation.

4

Value the book with what was known at the time

TimingEffect: high once NAV drives anythingEffort: M

Now

Fund.align_history uses a mark from its valuation date: the 31 December NAV is in the book on 31 December.

Gap

Private funds report 45 to 90 days after quarter end, and later for funds-of-funds and audited year-end accounts. On 31 December the investor knew the 30 September mark and the cash flows since. Using the December mark in December is look-ahead.

Under today's rule — sizing on the liquid balance only — this distorts the reported total value and the private share, not the commitments. It becomes decision-critical the moment anything is sized on total value or a target allocation (7) or an allocation limit is tested (5). It matters most in a fall: hindsight marks write the book down a quarter before the investor saw it, so the as-valued private share understates the squeeze the investor actually felt.

Change

One parameter on the rule the engine already uses:

as-valued (now)   nav(t) = latest mark dated ≤ t          + calls − distributions since that mark
as-known          nav(t) = latest mark dated ≤ t − lag    + calls − distributions since that mark

lag = 0 reproduces today's behaviour. Keep both series: as-known feeds SizingBalances.private_nav and every allocation test; as-valued stays in the report as the hindsight valuation. Default to 75 days, or use actual report dates per fund if the database has them.

Where

Fund.align_history (a second unit_nav_known array on AlignedFundHistory), step 4 of Simulator.run, and one more column in periods.

5

Measure liquidity the way it actually fails

LiquidityEffect: highEffort: M

Now

The only liquidity test is Shortfall: a period's calls exceed the entire liquid balance.

Gap

At commitment rates of 1–3% of the liquid balance a year the pot cannot plausibly reach zero, so the test never fires and says nothing about liquidity risk. Investors get into trouble much earlier: when unfunded commitments are large against liquid assets, when the private share breaches its limit after a public-market fall (the denominator effect of 2022), when distributions stop and calls don't.

Change

Track unfunded commitments and report, at every observation:

unfunded(t)       = Σ funds  max(0, commitment_usd − cumulative calls_usd) × fx[t]
private share(t)  = private_nav(t) / total(t)                         # as-known NAV, see 4
exposure(t)       = ( private_nav(t) + unfunded(t) ) / total(t)
coverage(t)       = liquid(t) / unfunded(t)

Flag breaches of a maximum private share and a minimum coverage as policy violations rather than waiting for cash to run out, and add unfunded to SizingBalances so a policy can see it. Where distributions are recallable, called capital exceeds 100% of commitment — size expectations from each fund's historical peak net cash outlay, not from the commitment. Then stress it with the path in 8 and report the worst coverage and the largest breach.

Where

CommitmentBook (cumulative calls per commitment), SizingBalances, new columns in periods.

6

Hold a cash sleeve, respect the notice period, pay to trade

LiquidityEffect: medium to highEffort: M

Now

The whole liquid balance earns the profile return every month and is sold, free, the day a call is due. Distributions are reinvested free the same day.

Gap

Investors keep a reserve for upcoming calls — a call notice gives about ten business days, and nobody wants to be a forced seller — and that reserve earns the cash rate, not the portfolio return. Selling to fund calls and reinvesting distributions costs a spread.

Drag ≈ reserve share × (portfolio return − cash return): a 3% reserve with a 4% return gap is 12 bp a year. Against a programme worth perhaps 30–45 bp a year to the whole portfolio — a 10–15% allocation earning 3% over the liquid portfolio — that is a quarter to a third of the benefit. Illustrative, but it's the right order of magnitude to care about.

Change

Split LiquidAccount into an invested sleeve and a cash sleeve with its own return series. A reserve rule tops the cash sleeve up to the next few months of expected calls; calls are paid from cash; transfers between sleeves pay a cost in basis points. Estimate expected calls from unfunded × a historical call rate — never from a fund's actual future calls, which would be look-ahead. Report cash drag as its own line.

Where

pmsim/state.py (LiquidAccount) and steps 2, 3 and 5 of Simulator.run; a cash-rate column in the Liquid or FX sheet.

7

Let the commitment policy react — and ask where the schedule came from

PolicyEffect: high for policy conclusionsEffort: M

Now

AnnualRatePolicy applies a fixed glide path — the Commitments sheet, by years since inception — to the liquid balance on the closing date.

Gap
  • Nobody follows a schedule blind. Real pacing looks at the private share against its target, at unfunded commitments and at liquidity, and slows or accelerates.
  • Provenance. If the schedule was calibrated on these same funds' cash flows — tuned until the allocation path looked right — the backtest is in-sample and will look better behaved than any forward use of the same schedule.
  • One date decides a vintage. Every PEM closing is the last day of December, so each commitment is sized on a single month-end balance. PEM2019 was sized on 31 December 2018, the low of a sharp quarter; PEM2022 and SEC_IX on 31 December 2021, within days of the peak. That pro-cyclicality is a true property of percent-of-portfolio rules; the single-date noise on top of it isn't.
Change

Add a target-allocation policy behind the existing CommitmentPolicy protocol, fed by as-known NAV (4) and unfunded commitments (5):

gap(t)        = target share × total(t) − ( private_nav(t) + k × unfunded(t) )
commitment(t) = min( max(0, speed × gap(t)),  cap × liquid(t) )

Run the fixed schedule and the adaptive rule side by side. For the schedule itself: write down how it was derived, and size on a trailing average of the balance — twelve months, say — instead of one month end.

Where

pmsim/policy.py: a new class. The engine doesn't change.

8

Roll the start date, and add the path the sample doesn't contain

RobustnessEffect: highEffort: S

Now

One run, starting a period before the first Liquid return: 31 March 2009.

Gap

That is three weeks after the bottom of the financial crisis, followed by the longest bull market on record. One path is one draw. And the sample contains no episode in which a mature private book meets a deep, prolonged public fall with distributions shut — the scenario liquidity planning exists for. It does contain 2022–24, a public fall with slow distributions: checking that the simulation reproduces that experience is the best in-sample test available.

Change

The schedule is keyed to years since inception, so a later start is just a later Year 0. Start the programme at every year end from 2009 to 2018, committing only to funds that close after the start, and report the spread of value added, PME, peak private share and worst coverage rather than one number. Then a constructed stress path: liquid down 30% over six months, distributions at a quarter of history for two years, calls unchanged.

Where

A loop outside the engine: slice the Liquid series and filter fund_specs to closings on or after the start (the simulator rejects earlier ones by design). The stress path is a modified Liquid series and scaled unit_distributions.

9

Smoothed marks understate risk

ValuationEffect: high if risk statistics are reportedEffort: M

Now

Between marks the engine holds NAV at the last mark adjusted for cash flows, with no return. On a monthly grid private value is flat for two months and jumps in the third; the marks themselves are appraisals that trail public markets.

Gap

Volatility, drawdown, Sharpe ratio and correlation taken from total_close flatter a portfolio in proportion to its private share. Reported private-equity volatility is typically half, or less, of what unsmoothed estimates give.

Change

Don't publish risk statistics from the raw series without saying so. Better, unsmooth quarterly private returns first:

r_true(t) = ( r_reported(t) − ρ × r_reported(t−1) ) / (1 − ρ)        # ρ: first-order autocorrelation

For a monthly economic view, roll NAV forward between marks with a public proxy (β × the listed-equity return) and true up at each mark — for reporting only; decisions keep as-known NAV. For liquidity stress, haircut NAV: secondary interests change hands at a discount to NAV, and a much wider one in a crisis.

Where

Post-processing on result.funds; optionally a proxy series in Fund.align_history.

10

The horizon hides the youngest vintages

ValuationEffect: mediumEffort: S

Now

Terminal value is the last liquid balance plus the last NAV.

Gap

Commitments scale with a portfolio that has grown for fifteen years, so the latest vintages are the largest in dollars, are still being called, and are carried near cost. Much of the terminal value added is unrealised and sits in funds too young to judge, and the headline is sensitive to the end date. The unfunded commitments outstanding at the horizon are a liability the terminal value ignores.

Change

Split value added into realised (distributions − calls, compounded) and unrealised (NAV). Report DPI, RVPI and TVPI by vintage. Show the headline again with vintages younger than four or five years excluded, and state the unfunded balance at the horizon beside it.

Where

Reporting only, from result.funds and result.commitments.

11

Say what is hedged, then model it

CurrencyEffect: medium to high for EUR and GBPEffort: M

Now

Calls are converted at spot when they are paid, so the currency risk on unfunded dollar commitments is genuinely captured. Private NAV is translated at spot: the private sleeve is fully unhedged.

Gap

If the EUR and GBP liquid profiles are currency-hedged, as conservative profiles usually are, the private sleeve becomes the portfolio's main dollar exposure and the comparison mixes a hedged portfolio with an unhedged one. Investors who hedge private NAV pay or earn the interest differential — between about 1.5% and 3% a year against euro investors in 2018–19 and 2023–24 — and settle the hedge in cash from the liquid portfolio, most painfully when the dollar rallies.

Change

State the hedging assumption for each profile. Optionally hedge a ratio h of as-known private NAV with rolling three-month forwards:

F         = S(0) × ( 1 + (r_base − r_usd) × τ )                  # base currency per USD
hedge P&L = h × nav_usd × ( F − S(τ) )                           # settled in cash on the liquid account
Where

SimulationSpec (hedge ratio, a short-rate or forward-points series per currency) and one settlement step in Simulator.run.

12

Client cash flows and minimum tickets

LiquidityEffect: medium for client-sized portfoliosEffort: S

Now

No external flows, and a commitment of any size is allowed.

Gap

For a private client the real liquidity strain is spending: a 3–4% annual withdrawal taken entirely from the liquid sleeve, while the private book is locked, raises the private share every year. And programmes have minimum commitments — 2.2% of a 2 million portfolio is 44,000, below most of them.

Change

An optional dated external-flow series, applied with distributions in step 3 (the original function's wealth_flows stub). A minimum-ticket rule in the policy: below the minimum, skip and carry the rate forward until the pooled amount clears it. Run the backtest at two or three realistic portfolio sizes as well as the index-style 100.

Where

One optional series on Portfolio, step 3 of Simulator.run, and AnnualRatePolicy.

13

Three small mechanics

MechanicsEffect: lowEffort: S each

FX

Convert each flow at the rate of the day it happened. Flows are pooled in dollars onto the month end and converted at the month-end rate; a call on the 7th was paid at the 7th's rate. EURUSD moves 2–3% in a typical month, so single flows are mistranslated by a percent or two — noise, not bias. When the database supplies daily FX:

now        calls_base[t] = commitment_usd × Σ unit_call(i) × fx[t]
proposed   calls_base[t] = commitment_usd × Σ unit_call(i) × fx[d(i)]

Commitment dollars are a scalar per fund, so this is a second pair of precomputed arrays on AlignedFundHistory; the loop is unchanged. With monthly FX only, leave it.

Lag

Size on the balance you knew. A fund closing on 31 December is sized on that day's balance, after December's return, though the commitment was decided and signed earlier. Same-bar look-ahead: tiny — a 2.2% commitment on a balance a few percent different — and unbiased on average, but free to remove. Pass the previous observation's close, or the trailing average from 7, as SizingBalances.liquid.

Dietz

Time-weight flows inside the period. The return is applied to the whole opening balance and flows settle at the period end, so cash called on the 3rd still earns the month in the liquid portfolio. Modified Dietz corrects it:

liquid_pnl(t) = r(t) × ( liquid_open + Σ w(i) × flow(i) )        w(i) = days left after flow i / days in period

With calls of 2–3% of the portfolio a year this is under 1 bp a year on a monthly grid and about three times that on a quarterly one. Do it for tidiness — Fund.events_by_day already has the dates — not for the numbers.

14

Keep the universe honest

ProcessNo code

Now

The backtest commits to every fund in the Spec sheet by rule, so nothing is selected on performance. That is the right design; the risk is in what the sheet contains.

Keep
  • Every programme that was actually offered, including any that were small, merged or disappointing — fixed by what was investable on each date, not by what is on the shelf today.
  • Unit cash flows of an investor who committed on the Spec sheet's date: a first-close investor pays no equalisation, a later one does.
  • A complete list: no BUYOUT fund closes in 2024 or 2025 in the current Spec sheet.

What the engine already gets right

Worth keeping exactly as it is while the rest changes.

  • Actual fund cash flows, not a model. The J-curve, the pace of calls and the dispersion between vintages are real.
  • Gross calls and distributions kept apart, so a liquidity need is never hidden by netting.
  • Commitments fixed in dollars at the closing rate, calls converted when paid. The currency risk on unfunded commitments is captured.
  • Between marks, NAV is the last mark adjusted for cash flows — the roll-forward investors themselves use — and a mark is taken to include that day's flows.
  • One sizing balance per closing date. Funds closing together are sized from the same figure, and a fund's own distributions can't inflate its own commitment.
  • Flows pool onto the next pricing date, never the previous one. No cash flow is used before it happened.
  • No silent drops. Funds beyond the horizon are listed; a fund closing before inception is an error, not an assumption.
  • Every return is applied. The run starts one period before the first return, rolled back to a business day.

A sensible order of work

Reporting first, because it costs nothing and shows which of the rest matter on your data.

  1. Confirm carry-forward. A setting, today.
  2. Reporting, no engine change: counterfactual and PME (1, done), realised and unrealised by vintage (10), rolling starts (8). After this the backtest gives an answer and a spread around it.
  3. One question, one parameter: the fee basis of the Liquid returns (2).
  4. Honest inputs: as-known NAV (4), then unfunded, exposure, coverage and limits (5). In that order, so the limits are tested on what was known.
  5. Frictions: cash sleeve and trading costs (6); client flows and minimum tickets (12).
  6. Policy: adaptive pacing and trailing-average sizing (7). It now has honest inputs to react to.
  7. Risk and currency: unsmoothing and the risk-matched counterfactual (9, 3); hedging (11).
  8. When the database brings daily data: the small mechanics (13).
