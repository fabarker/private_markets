pmsim · design note for review

Commitment Draw Plans

A way to say, per fund, which of the pacing model's yearly commitments it collects and at what multiple — so one secondaries subscription can take four vintages' worth of budget, or three times a single year's. Reviewed, decided and built; this note is kept as the record of why it works the way it does.

As built

Every decision in §10 came back the same way I recommended it, with one confirmation that mattered: combining a set of years means combining the dollar commitments those years compute to — each year priced on its own rate and its own portfolio value, then added — not pooling rates or percentages. That is what the engine does.

  • ×3 is a multiplier on one year, not three consecutive years.
  • Convention b: each drawn year's rate is normalised on its own plan date.
  • The plan lives in a Draws column on the Spec sheet — 1-4, 12x3 — and an explicit plan switches carry-forward off for that fund type.

The code landed close to the estimate: Entitlement is now (policy_year, fund_type, weight, draws), carry-forward and a plan are two ways of filling the same {year: multiplier} mapping, and result.draws is the per-drawn-year audit table of §8. Old behaviour is unchanged to the bit: across six sample runs the periods and funds tables are byte-identical to before, and every commitment matches to 0.000e+00.

The figures in §5 were the prediction. The run gives SEC_VI 2.930, SEC_VII 3.819, SEC_VIII 3.802, SEC_IX 4.555, SEC_X 5.389, 20.495 for the sleeve — the plan's numbers to three figures.

1 · What you asked for

Five secondaries funds, each collecting a named block of the pacing model's yearly commitments.

Relative years run from inception: the sample workbook's first Liquid date is in 2009, so year k is calendar 2009 + k, and year 0 carries no commitment. Against that, the five subscriptions and the years you want each to claim:

Two things fall straight out of that table. First, every fund but the last two claims years after its own closing — which the engine cannot do today, and which is the one genuinely new mechanic here (§3). Second, the mapping is a deliberate pattern, not a consequence of the closing dates, so it has to be stated as data rather than derived.

Buyout needs none of this: PEM2011…PEM2024 close in every year from 2010 to 2023, so each simply takes its own year and nothing waits. This feature is, in practice, about the secondaries sleeve.

Settled: reading A

“Year 12 × 3” means three times year 12's commitment. Both readings were plausible and they give different answers. On your own Commitments sheet's USD Conservative secondaries rates (flat 0.80% through year 7, rising after):

The grammar expresses either reading at no extra cost — B would be 12-14 instead of 12x3 — so switching is a spreadsheet edit, not a code change.

2 · The idea: a fund draws years

One concept covers what the engine does today and everything you have just asked for.

A fund draws a set of schedule years, each with a multiplier. That is the whole mechanic. The policy already settles which years a fund collects before the run starts and leaves the dollars to the run (_entitlements in policy.py); this widens that set from “its own year, plus whatever was carried to it” to “whatever you say”, and attaches a multiplier.

Today's two behaviours become the two default ways of filling that set:

RuleYears drawnStatus
carry_forward=False{closing year: 1}today, unchanged
carry_forward=True{closing year: 1, each waiting year: 1}today, unchanged
a draw planwhatever the plan says, with multipliersnew

So this is a generalisation rather than a parallel mechanism: one code path, three ways of arriving at the same {year: multiplier} mapping. A fund with no plan behaves exactly as it does now, which is what keeps the change small and the 200 existing tests meaningful.

It is worth naming what the feature models. A secondaries subscription bought in 2011 that takes 2010–2013's budget is buying four vintages of exposure in one commitment — the pacing model's yearly cadence and the fund calendar's lumpiness reconciled by hand. Vintage-year aggregation is the thing; a draw plan is how you write it down.

3 · Drawing forward, without look-ahead

The engine deliberately hides the future from the policy. That guard has to stay.

Commitment sizing today is scrupulous about information. Simulator._sizing_balances hands the policy only completed calendar years:

year_ends={year: end for year, end in self._year_ends.items() if year < day.year},

The simulator holds every year end for the whole run — it computed them all up front — and that filter is the single thing stopping a commitment being sized on a balance the investor could not have known. A fund closing on 31 Dec 2011 that claims year 2013's budget must not be sized on 31 Dec 2013's liquid value. The filter stays exactly as it is.

Which leaves a straightforward rule. A commitment is fixed once, on the closing date, so that is the latest date anything about it can be known:

Rule

A drawn year is funded from its own year end when the run has reached it, and from the closing observation otherwise. Equivalently: the year end of min(drawn year, closing year).

For a fund that draws only its own year and earlier ones, this is precisely what the engine does now — so carry-forward keeps its current numbers to the last decimal.

A year before the first observation still contributes nothing: there was no portfolio to size on, and year_end_on_or_before already returns None there.

4 · Plan date and funding date

Forward draws split one date into two, and the split changes the answer. This is the part I'd most like you to check.

Recall how a rate becomes a share. The Commitments sheet is a pacing schedule quoted per 1 of liquid value on the first commitment date, in a model where the portfolio then grows at X, so the engine divides it back out before using it:

expected_value(d) = (1 + X) ** years from the first commitment date to d
share             = schedule[year, type] / expected_value(d)
budget            = share × actual liquid-only value in USD

Until now d has been unambiguous: a year's rate was always divided by the expected value on that year's date, and multiplied by the liquid value on that same date. A forward draw prises the two apart, and they answer different questions:

DateAnswersFor a forward draw
plan datewhat fraction of the portfolio did the pacing model intend for this year?the drawn year's own date — the model's calendar, not an observation
funding datewhat is the portfolio actually worth when we write the cheque?the closing observation

Take the two candidate conventions. Year 4's rate in the sample workbook is 0.937%, and SEC_VI closes at the end of year 2, when the model expects 1.054:

ConventionDivides the rate byShare of liquid value
a — one date for bothexpected_value(closing) = 1.0540.889%
b — plan date for the rateexpected_value(end of year 4) = 1.1710.800%

Convention a quietly inflates every forward draw by the expected growth between the two dates. The pacing model earmarked 0.937% of the larger portfolio it expects in year 4; charging that number against today's smaller portfolio commits more than the model ever intended — 11.1% more for a draw two years out at X = 5.4%, and the error compounds with the reach.

Recommendation

Convention b. Normalise each drawn year's rate by the expected value on that year's own date, then fund it from the liquid value on the funding date. Every drawn year then contributes the share the pacing model meant it to, and the only thing the closing date decides is what that share is applied to.

The sample workbook makes this visible: its schedule grows at exactly X, so under b every drawn year resolves to 0.800% on the nose, and SEC_VI's four years come to 0.753 + 3 × 0.726 = $2.93. Under a the same four years give $3.05.

One practical detail: the plan date for a year the run has not reached cannot be an observation, so it is 31 December of that year — a date on the pacing model's own calendar, which touches no market data and therefore leaks nothing. For a month-end series it is the same date the observation would have carried anyway, so past and future years are treated consistently:

def _plan_date(self, year, balances):
    """Where the pacing model puts a year: its last observation, or 31 December when the run has not reached it."""
    year_end = balances.year_ends.get(year)
    return year_end.date if year_end is not None else date(year, 12, 31)

Three things worth noting before you commit to the mapping:

  • SEC_VI roughly doubles. It goes from claiming two years to claiming four, at the bottom of the portfolio's value range (liquid-only was 94.13 at end-2010 and 90.70 at end-2011, both below the $100 start). That is the largest single change the plan makes.
  • SEC_X shrinks by a fifth, from 6.57 to 5.39: today's carry-forward hands it four years' rates (13–16), the plan gives it three times year 16's. The ×3 multiplier is smaller than the accumulation it replaces.
  • The programme total barely moves — 20.50 against 19.24, up 7% — but the timing shifts materially earlier. More capital committed in 2011 and 2015 means more called sooner, a deeper J-curve, and a different PME. The counterfactual and PME tables will tell you whether that helps.

The two conventions differ by 2.4% at the programme level and only for the three funds that reach forward, which is small — but it is a systematic over-commitment, not noise, and it grows with X and with reach.

6 · Where the plan lives

Per-fund data, so it belongs with the fund spec. Three ways to put it there.

A · A Draws column on the Spec sheet recommended

One cell per fund, the whole plan visible beside the closing dates.

NameYearTypeDraws
SEC_VI31/12/2011SECONDARIES1-4
SEC_VII31/12/2015SECONDARIES5-8
SEC_VIII31/12/2018SECONDARIES9-11
SEC_IX31/12/2021SECONDARIES12x3
SEC_X31/12/2025SECONDARIES16x3
PEM201131/12/2010BUYOUT—

A grammar small enough to hold in your head: comma-separated terms, each a year (7), a run of years (1-4), or a year with a multiplier (12x3). So 1-4, 7 and 12x3, 15 both work. Blank means “behave as now”. Years are relative, matching the Commitments sheet and your own description; the existing Year column holds the closing date, so there is no collision.

Because Spec is shared across profiles, one plan governs all nine currency/risk combinations — right, since the fund universe is shared and only the rates differ.

B · A Draws sheet, one row per fund-year

More robust, more rows, nothing to parse: Name | Year | Multiplier.

SEC_VI becomes four rows. Sixteen rows for the sleeve rather than five cells. Worth it if the plans ever grow fractional multipliers or per-profile variants, since a column of numbers beats a grammar for that. Otherwise the compact form shows the pattern and this hides it.

C · In code, as a mapping

Exists either way — it is the engine's own interface.

AnnualRatePolicy(rates, funds, draws={# relative years
    "SEC_VI": {1: 1, 2: 1, 3: 1, 4: 1},
    "SEC_IX": {12: 3},
})

A SimulationSpec.draws field overrides whatever the workbook says, the way commitment_rates and expected_return already do — which is what makes the feature testable without touching a spreadsheet.

I'd build A, with C underneath it and B available later if the grammar ever gets in the way. The parser belongs in data/tables.py beside the other normalisers, so policy.py only ever sees a parsed {year: multiplier} mapping and stays free of data-entry concerns.

7 · What changes

Five files, no new modules, no new dependencies. Around 250 lines including tests.

pmsim/policy.py
Entitlement collapses to (policy_year, weight, draws), where draws is {calendar year: multiplier} — the closing year is simply one entry, so current_year_rate and carried_year_rates both disappear into it (the reported columns are derived, §8). _entitlements gains a third mode; _budgets_usd loops over the draws, picking a plan date and a funding date for each. ~60 lines.
pmsim/data/tables.py
parse_draw_plan: the grammar of option A, relative years in, an error naming the fund and the offending term when it doesn't parse. ~35 lines.
pmsim/data/repository.py
fund_specs() reads an optional Draws column and passes it through as typed text; normalize_fund_specs keeps it. Absent column → absent plans → today's behaviour. ~10 lines.
pmsim/data/orchestrator.py
Converts relative years to calendar years — the same inception_year offset calendar_rates_for_profile already applies — and hands the result to the policy. ~10 lines.
pmsim/data/spec.py
A draws field, so a run can be given a plan without a workbook. ~3 lines.
pmsim/simulator.py
Two columns renamed in COMMITMENT_COLUMNS and the new draws table on SimulationResult. ~15 lines.

The sketch, to show the shape rather than the finished code:

def _entitlements(rates, groups, weights, carry_forward, plans):
    entitlements = {}
    for fund_type in rates.columns:
        waiting: dict[int, float] = {}
        for year in rates.index:
            cohort = groups.get((int(year), fund_type), [])
            for fund in cohort:
                # an explicit plan wins outright; otherwise its own year plus whatever waited
                draws = plans.get(fund.name) or {int(year): 1.0, **waiting}
                entitlements[fund.name] = Entitlement(int(year), weights[fund.name], draws)
            if cohort:
                waiting = {}
            elif carry_forward and float(rates.at[year, fund_type]) > 0:
                waiting[int(year)] = 1.0
    return entitlements


def _budgets_usd(self, fund_name, balances):
    entitlement = self.entitlements[fund_name]
    amounts = []
    for year, multiplier in entitlement.draws.items():
        # funded from the year's own end, or from today when the run has not reached it
        funding = balances.year_end_on_or_before(min(year, entitlement.policy_year))
        if funding is None:
            continue                                # a year before the run began has nothing to size on
        # but normalised by what the pacing model planned for that year, not for today
        share = self.rates.at[year, ...] / self.expected_value(self._plan_date(year, balances), first)
        amounts.append(multiplier * share * funding.liquid_only_usd)
    return math.fsum(amounts)

Two risks I can see. The first is that Entitlement is on the public surface, so collapsing its fields is a breaking change to anything reading carried_year_rates — inside this package that is only explain_commitment and the policy tests, but say the word and I'll keep the old fields as derived properties. The second is that min(year, policy_year) is the whole no-look-ahead guarantee; it wants a test that fails loudly if a future year's balance ever reaches a commitment, not just one that checks the arithmetic.

8 · What it reports

A plan you cannot audit is worse than no plan.

The commitments table keeps its shape; carried_years and carried_usd generalise to drawn_years ("2010, 2011, 2012, 2013") and own_year_usd / other_years_usd. But the interesting detail is per draw, not per fund, so I'd add a long-form table — one row for every year every fund draws:

Every number that went into a commitment, in one place, with the two dates side by side so a forward draw is visible as the row where they differ. This is also the table that makes convention a versus b a two-second check rather than an argument.

Alongside it, a coverage report per fund type — which schedule years were drawn, by whom, at what multiple, and which were never drawn at all. For reading A that report would name years 13–15 and 17–20 as unspent, which is either the point of the plan or a typo in it, and only you can say which.

Weights

Weights and draw plans are orthogonal and compose cleanly: a plan says which years a fund draws, weights split a year among funds of one type closing together. A fund's commitment stays weight × Σ draws. Since no two secondaries close in the same year here, weights are all 1.0 and nothing changes — but the composition should be a test rather than an accident.

10 · Decisions

Answer these and I'll build it. My own answer is first in each case.

  1. “× 3” — multiplier or three years? I've assumed a multiplier (reading A). The grammar supports both, so this only decides what goes in the cells.
  2. Convention b for forward draws? Normalise each drawn year by its own plan date, not by the closing date. I recommend b; a is 2.4% richer at the programme level and systematically so.
  3. Are years 13–15 and 17–20 meant to go unspent? Under reading A the secondaries schedule stops being drawn after year 16. Fine if the programme really ends there — but if those years should land somewhere, the plan needs more funds or wider ranges.
  4. Does an explicit plan switch carry-forward off for that fund type? I'd say yes: once you have named the years, having the engine also pool leftovers behind your back defeats the point. Buyout would keep carry_forward as it is, and in practice never needs it.
  5. Option A, B or C for where the plan lives? I'd put it in a Draws column on Spec, with the code-level mapping underneath for tests and overrides.

One last thing worth flagging, which is yours to judge rather than mine. Letting a 2011 subscription draw 2013's budget means the backtest commits capital against a pacing allowance that, in the real world, hadn't been granted yet. The dollars are honest — nothing is sized on a balance the investor couldn't see — but the decision is one only a committee willing to pre-spend its own pacing plan would take. That is a fair description of how secondaries subscriptions actually get approved, so I think it's realistic. It's worth writing down as a stated assumption of the backtest rather than leaving it implicit in a spreadsheet column.
