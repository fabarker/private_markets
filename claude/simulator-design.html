Private Markets Simulator Design

The object design for the job the original generate_cash_flows_liquid_private did in one function, built around the data the system receives: a dated liquid index in the portfolio's base currency, specific future funds with closing dates and unit histories in US dollars, annual commitment rates by fund type, and the exchange rates that join the two. Implemented as the pmsim package in this folder.

  • 2 input objects: Fund and Portfolio
  • 1 loop, in Simulator.run()
  • 1 currency boundary, crossed in one place
  • dates converted to periods once, by a Timeline
  • pmsim · 7 engine modules + a data layer · 198 tests

The job

Two pots of money in two currencies, one of which feeds the other.

An investor holds a liquid portfolio in a base currency — sterling, euros, dollars — whose value is given as a total-return level on a set of dates. They have a pipeline of private funds, each with a type, a closing date, and a history of what one dollar committed to it called, distributed and was worth over time. On each fund's closing the investor commits a slice of the liquid pot, sized by that year's rate for that fund type. From then on the fund's unit history, scaled by the dollars committed and translated at the exchange rate of the day, drives cash out of the liquid pot (calls) and back in (distributions), and gives the private NAV in base currency.

The simulator projects the liquid balance, the private NAV, and every flow between the pots on the liquid index's own dates, and stops if the pot ever cannot meet a call. The original function had the essential idea — a unit profile scaled by a commitment — but ran on annual array positions, in one currency, with all the bookkeeping in three nested loops. The design below keeps the idea and gives each part of the bookkeeping a home.

Portfolio  one · base currency

liquid_levels
Dated total-return levels in base currency. The dates are the simulation grid; the first level is the starting balance.
commitment_rates
DataFrame, calendar year × fund_type: the pacing schedule. With the portfolio's expected return it is turned into shares of the liquid-only value (see Commitment sizing); without one it is taken to hold such shares already. Every year from first to last observation, 0 for no target.
usd_rate
Dated price of 1 USD in base currency. Required when base_currency is not USD; rejected when it is. May be sparse.

Each history may be given as a Series indexed by dates, a {date: value} mapping, or (date, value) pairs; both objects copy their inputs and validate them at construction. The commitment dollars are not an input. They are the engine's decision, made at each closing, and they live in the result — a Fund is never mutated.

Principles

Six rules that decide most of the design.

Inputs mirror ownership

What the fund knows lives on Fund; what the portfolio knows lives on Portfolio. No third bag of parameters.

Dates in, periods inside

A Timeline built from the liquid dates converts every dated input to per-period arrays once. The loop then works on integer t and never touches a date.

Unit data × a dollar commitment

Fund behaviour stays per $1 committed. Dollar figures are always commitment_usd × unit[t]. That was the original's own convention.

One currency boundary

Private figures are always USD; the liquid pot and reports are in base_currency. That setting alone decides whether conversion happens. The rate is applied in the loop, at the observation date, and nowhere else.

Policy is a plug

Sizing is one method: given the funds closing now and the balances in US dollars, return dollar commitments. Carry-forward, weights, pacing targets are all policies.

One loop, plain objects

Only Simulator.run() steps through time. Frozen dataclasses for data, one Protocol, no inheritance. Results are pandas tables.

The objects

Ten small classes in five stages, as built. Colour marks the currency side: USD / fund, base currency / portfolio, and the engine that joins them.

Who holds whom

  • Simulator takes one Portfolio, many Funds and one CommitmentPolicy. From the portfolio it derives a Timeline, the period returns, the liquid-only path and an exchange-rate array; from each fund a AlignedFundHistory.
  • CommitmentBook owns many Commitments; each references its Fund and AlignedFundHistory. Paths are immutable, prepared once, and shared.
  • The policy sees a SizingBalances snapshot and the closing cohort, never the portfolios. Sizing is pure and testable without a simulator.
  • Nothing references the Simulator. The result is a value; the simulator can be discarded after run(), or run again for an identical result.

Dates become periods

Three conversions, all done by Timeline and Fund.align_history() before the loop starts.

Closings

closing_period = timeline.first_observation_on_or_after(closing_date) — the first observation on or after the closing. A fund closing on 15 February is committed at the 31 March observation. Its rate year is still the calendar year of the actual closing date. A closing after the last observation gets closing_period = n and is never committed; it is listed in result.funds_beyond_horizon, not dropped, and still counts in its year's weight split. A closing before the first observation is rejected: there are no pre-existing commitments.

Flows

Unit calls and distributions dated in (dates[t−1], dates[t]] are summed into period t. The first period takes everything on or before the first date. Calls and distributions are bucketed separately, so a same-day call and distribution both survive.

Unit NAV

Walk the fund's events in date order with a running unit NAV starting at 0: each call adds, each distribution subtracts, and a NAV mark replaces the running value (marks are assumed to include that day's flows). path.unit_nav[t] is the running value after the last event on or before dates[t]. A mark between observations therefore counts; a missing mark just leaves the cash-adjusted estimate in place; a negative running value is a data error raised at preparation, naming the fund and date.

Exchange rates

fx = timeline.last_value_on_or_before(portfolio.usd_rate): the last rate on or before each observation. The rate series may be daily or sparse; it must have a value on or before the first observation.

Observation frequency

The liquid index sets the grid. Fund events fall on whatever day they happened. One rule covers every mismatch.

A total-return series is typically priced at month ends, or quarter ends, or every business day; fund calls, distributions and NAV marks occur on any day in between. The system does not resample either side. It applies one rule to every mismatch of periodicity, date or pricing convention:

The rule

An event on any day pools onto the first observation on or after that day. Intramonth flows land on that month's end on a monthly grid; intra-quarter flows on that quarter end; a Saturday flow on the next business day of a daily grid. Totals are consistent across frequencies: a quarter's calls on a quarterly grid equal the sum of its three months' calls on a monthly grid, and unit NAV at a shared date is identical on both.

Exchange rates go the other way because a rate is a state rather than an event: each observation uses the last rate known on or before it. Simulator.map_events_to_observations() lists every fund event with the observation it pooled onto, for audit; events after the last observation show no observation and are ignored by the run.

The consequence to be aware of: because flows settle at observations, a call dated the 3rd and one dated the 28th are treated alike within the month. Neither loses nor earns that month's return, and a liquidity shortfall is detected at the month end, not on the day. A finer liquid grid makes the simulation finer; the rule does not change.

Currency

Private data is always in US dollars; the liquid pot and the reports are in base currency. The boundary is crossed in exactly four lines of the loop.

QuantityNative currencyConversion
Sizing base, commitment decisionUSDSizing happens exclusively in dollars. The liquid-only value is converted first, liquid_only_usd = liquid_only ÷ fx[t], and the policy sees and returns USD only: share × liquid_only_usd. Private NAV reaches it untranslated, and is not used for sizing.
Commitment amountUSD, fixed at closingDecided in dollars and never changed afterwards. commitment_base = commitment_usd × fx[closing_period] is that day's translation, reported for information; its base-currency value floats from then on.
Calls, distributions in a periodUSDMultiplied by fx[t] on the observation they settle on, then applied to the liquid pot.

Two facts are fixed in the system rather than supplied per input: private-markets flows and NAV are always in US dollars (a module constant, PRIVATE_CURRENCY = "USD"), and the liquid index is always in Portfolio.base_currency. That one setting therefore tells the system whether to convert at all. When it is "USD" the rate array is all ones and a supplied usd_rate is rejected as contradictory; when it is anything else usd_rate is required. Both checks run when the Portfolio is constructed, before any simulator exists. The rate is quoted as base currency per 1 USD, so USD figures are multiplied to reach base and base figures divided to reach USD. Intra-period flows are converted at the observation-date rate, consistent with all flows settling at the observation.

Commitment sizing

Three rules decide every commitment. Together they mean the liquid returns, the initial value, the exchange rates and the pacing schedule are all the system needs to size all of them.

In US dollars

Private funds are committed to in dollars, so the decision is made in dollars whatever the base currency. The engine converts before it asks the policy, and base-currency figures are that day's translation, reported and never decided on.

On the liquid-only value

The initial value compounded by the liquid returns — the level series itself — with no capital call or distribution in it. Private assets never interfere: the account that pays the calls is a different number, used for the shortfall test and never for sizing. The same inputs give the same commitments whatever the funds then call or distribute, and a test asserts exactly that.

As a share of the expected liquid value

The commitment rates come from a pacing model in which the liquid portfolio is worth 1 on the day of the first commitment and then grows at its expected return X. A schedule entry is an amount per 1 of liquid value on that day, not a share of the value in its own year; it rises because the modelled portfolio grows. It is turned back into a share before it is used:

expected_value(d) = (1 + X) ^ years from the first commitment date to d      # exactly 1 on that date
share(d)          = schedule[year, type] / expected_value(d)                 # no unit: X and the schedule are in the portfolio's own currency
budget_usd        = share(d) × liquid-only value in USD on d
                  = schedule × (actual liquid value / expected liquid value) × the dollars the portfolio was worth on the first commitment date

The run commits the model's planned amount, scaled by how far the actual liquid value is ahead of, or behind, the expected one. Years are counted in anniversaries, so 31 December to 31 December is exactly 1 and the model's annual steps are hit exactly; between them the elapsed share of the year is used. Applying the schedule to the current value without this step would count the growth twice, by a factor of (1 + X)^years.

X is one number per portfolio, in the workbook's Liquid Spec sheet (Liquid | ExRet, the cells formatted as percentages so 5.4% is stored and read as 0.054). The sheet is required by WorkbookRepository — the schedule means nothing without the return it assumed — and SimulationSpec(expected_return=…) overrides it for a run. A policy built by hand without expected_return takes its table to hold shares already, which is what the worked example below does.

The starting value is not a setting

Every run assembled from data starts with 100,000,000 of the portfolio's own base currency: pmsim.STARTING_VALUE, a constant. SimulationSpec has no field for it; load_profile_workbook, simulation_spec and returns_to_levels take no starting amount, and passing one is a TypeError. It is always base currency — a EUR profile starts with 100,000,000 euros — and there is no way to start from an amount of another currency converted in; the script function that once did that is gone. One place applies it, build_portfolio: a returns series is compounded from 100,000,000 at the inception date, and a level series is read as an index and rescaled so its first level is 100,000,000. The engine class Portfolio still takes whatever levels it is handed, which is what the hand-checkable worked example below uses.

Market values on every period row

After its fixed columns, every row of result.periods carries the market value (NAV) of each fund type and of each fund, each in US dollars and in base currency: BUYOUT_total_nav_usd, BUYOUT_total_nav_base, SECONDARIES_total_nav_usd, …, then PEM2011_nav_usd, PEM2011_nav_base, and so on in the order the funds were given. The base-currency figure is the dollar figure at that observation's exchange rate, a fund is worth zero until it is committed, and the fund-type totals in base currency add up to private_close — an identity checked on every run in the test suite. The column names are built by two functions in simulator.py and nowhere else; result.market_values() returns just these columns.

Rounded the way the spreadsheet rounds

Each drawn year's dollar commitment is rounded to the nearest multiple of a rounding unit, halves away from zero — Excel's ROUND, which Python's own round is not: round sends 25,000 to 20,000, Excel sends it to 30,000, and so does round_like_excel. The division is done in decimal arithmetic, so 2.675 rounds to 2.68 as it does in Excel rather than being caught by its binary form. A run always starts from 100,000,000 (below), and the unit that goes with it is 10,000 dollars: COMMITMENT_ROUNDING_UNIT_USD, which is ROUND(value, -4). A year is rounded before its multiplier and the fund's weight, so 12x3 is three times one rounded amount. WorkbookRepository.simulation_spec applies the unit by default; commitment_rounding_unit_usd=None switches it off, and result.draws shows each year before and after rounding.

The five running values

The loop tracks, and result.tracked_values() reports, the running value of each thing the simulation holds, in base currency: 1 liquid_only, the liquid portfolio alone, which no call or distribution touches and which commitments are sized on; 2 expected_liquid, that same starting value growing at the expected return instead of at the market's; 3 liquid_close, the liquid account as it really stands; 4 private_close, the private book at its marks; and 5 total_close, the two together. The first three start from the same value at the first observation, so 1 against 2 is how far the portfolio ran ahead of or behind expectation, and 1 against 3 is what the private programme has taken out of the liquid account and not yet given back.

Which years a fund draws

The schedule budgets by year; funds close when they close. A fund therefore collects a set of schedule years, each with a multiplier — its draw plan — and every year it draws is priced as its own dollar commitment, by the three rules above, before the dollars are added. Dollars are combined, never rates or percentages. There are three ways a fund gets its years:

SettingYears drawnFor
carry_forward=False{closing year: 1}a type that closes a fund every year
carry_forward=True{closing year: 1} plus every year of the type that passed without a closinga type that closes irregularly and should spend everything
draws={fund: {year: multiplier}}exactly what the plan says, including years after the closinga subscription that buys several vintages at once

With carry-forward, a year in which no fund of a type closes is still sized, at that year's last observation: its own share, of its own liquid-only value, at its own expected value. The dollars then wait, and accumulate, until the next year that has a fund of the type, whose funds collect weight × (own_year_usd + other_years_usd).

A draw plan states the years instead. It comes from an optional Draws column on the workbook's Spec sheet, written in years since inception like the Commitments sheet: 1-4 draws years 1 to 4 once each, 12x3 draws three times year 12's commitment, and 1-4, 7 combines terms. Blank means "keep the default years". Naming any fund of a type switches carry-forward off for that whole type — once the years are named, pooling the rest behind them would commit dollars nobody asked for. SimulationSpec(draws=…) overrides the sheet, in calendar years.

Every year at its own year end — even one still to come

A drawn year is priced on one date, its sizing date: the pacing model's expected value and the liquid-only value are both taken there. For a year before the closing that is the year's own year end, as carry-forward has always done. For the fund's own closing year it is the closing observation. For a year after the closing it is, again, that year's own year end — which the run has not reached, so it looks ahead and uses the liquid value the portfolio will have then.

That is a deliberate choice, not an accident of implementation. It makes a fund that draws 1-4 collect exactly the four dollar commitments the schedule computes for those four years: a test asserts that a year drawn ahead equals, to the cent, what a fund closing in that year would have got. The price is hindsight in the backtest — SEC_VI is committed on 31 December 2011 and its size depends on the portfolio's value at the end of 2013. On the sample (USD Conservative, unrounded) it moves SEC_VI by +2.7%, SEC_VII by +6.3% and SEC_VIII by +7.3% against pricing them on the closing day; no other commitment changes, and a run without draw plans is unaffected.

The hindsight is kept visible and contained. SizingBalances.year_ends still holds completed calendar years only, and carry-forward reads nothing else. The year ends still to come travel separately, in SizingBalances.future_year_ends, and the only reader is the branch of drawn_years() for a year after the closing. Every row of result.draws priced that way has looks_ahead = True. A plan naming a year the liquid series does not reach is an error: there is no value to look ahead to, and another year's is never substituted.

The period loop

The original's cash ordering, kept. Numbered because the order is the contract.

  1. Snapshot the opening state
    liquid_open = liquid.balance  ·  nav_usd_open = book.nav_at(t − 1)  ·  private_open = last period's private_close
    Opening private NAV is last period's closing NAV in base currency, at last period's rate. At t = 0 both are zero: there are no pre-existing commitments.
  2. Apply the liquid return
    pnl = liquid.apply_return(t)  ·  balance × returns[t]  ·  returns[t] = level[t] / level[t − 1] − 1
    The account runs off the period return itself — the percent change between consecutive observations — and the first period, having no preceding observation, returns 0. No annualising, no assumed number of periods per year.
  3. Bank distributions from existing commitments
    distributions = book.distributions_in_period(t) × fx[t]  ·  liquid.deposit(distributions)
    Only commitments made before this observation exist yet, so this can never include a fund being sized in step 4.
  4. Size and fix commitments for the closing cohort
    cohort = funds with closing_period == t  ·  balances = SizingBalances(t, date, liquid_only[t] ÷ fx[t], …)  ·  for fund, dollars in policy.size_commitments(cohort, balances): book.add(Commitment(fund, path, dollars))
    All funds closing at this observation are sized in one call, in US dollars, on the liquid-only value: the initial value compounded by the liquid returns, which no call or distribution ever touches. The engine converts it before it asks, so a percentage, a fixed dollar ticket and a dollar minimum all mean the same thing whatever the base currency, and every commitment is known from the liquid returns, the initial value, the exchange rates and the schedule alone. A commitment creates a book entry; it moves no cash. The policy's output is checked: finite, non-negative, only for funds closing now.
  5. Bank the new cohort's distributions, then pay this period's calls
    liquid.deposit(Σ new.distributions_in_period(t) × fx[t])  ·  calls = book.calls_in_period(t) × fx[t]  ·  missing = liquid.withdraw(calls)
    A fund that distributes inside its own closing period could not be in step 3 (it did not exist yet); its distributions are banked here, after sizing, so they can help pay calls without inflating the base they were sized from. Calls include the cohort's, as in the original. Aggregated before withdrawal, so fund order cannot change the outcome.
  6. Value, record, and stop if the pot ran dry
    private_close = book.nav_at(t) × fx[t]  ·  append period, fund and commitment rows  ·  if missing > cash_tolerance and stop_on_shortfall: break
    The failed period is recorded with its negative balance visible and named in result.shortfall. With stop_on_shortfall=False the balance goes negative and the run continues — the "how much would I have to borrow" view. Exactly zero cash is valid, and an empty account does not shrink the next commitment: that is sized on the liquid-only value.

Accounting identities, asserted by the tests on every period of every run:

liquid_close = liquid_open + liquid_pnl + distributions − calls
private_valuation_pnl = private_close − private_open − calls + distributions
total_close − total_open = liquid_pnl + private_valuation_pnl

Fund A's dollar NAV fell from 20,625 to 16,500 only because it distributed 4,125 — no valuation change. But in sterling its NAV fell by more than the distribution, because the pound strengthened: that extra −1,031.25 is the fx_translation row, and it is the entire private valuation P&L this period. The same funds with base_currency="USD" give the single-currency figures: commitments 66,000 and 48,400, closing total 1,208,350.

Fund B is sized on the liquid-only value, 1,210,000 — the index itself — and not on the account, which by 30 June has paid A's call and banked its distribution and stands at 1,194,943.75. The account is what pays B's call; it is never what B is sized on.

Benchmarking a run

Every call is paid by selling the liquid portfolio and every distribution buys it back, so a run already is a public-market-equivalent calculation against the investor's own portfolio.

Two read-only methods on SimulationResult report it. They are computed in benchmark.py from the periods and funds tables alone — the liquid index is compounded from period_return — so nothing in the loop is involved. compare_with_liquid_only() sets total_close beside the same liquid portfolio with no private programme. public_market_equivalent() gives, for the programme, each fund type and each fund: the flows compounded to the last observation by the index, the value added, the Kaplan–Schoar PME, the IRR, and direct alpha (the IRR of the index-compounded flows: the yearly rate of out- or under-performance). With I the liquid index and T the last observation, the link between the two is exact:

with_programme(T) − liquid_only(T) = Σ (distributions(t) − calls(t)) × I(T)/I(t)  +  private_close(T)
                                   = fv_calls × (ks_pme − 1)

The programme's value_added equals the comparison's last row, and the funds' and fund types' add up to it; the tests assert all three, including through a shortfall. Private NAV counts at its carrying value, so part of any value added is unrealised. Ratios are NaN where they are undefined: nothing called yet, or every flow on one date.

Code skeleton

The built code, trimmed to signatures, the date-to-period sweep and the loop. Validation and reporting elided.

# inputs.py
PRIVATE_CURRENCY = "USD"                 # fund flows and NAV are always in dollars

@dataclass(frozen=True, eq=False)
class Fund:
    name: str; fund_type: str; closing_date: date
    unit_calls: Series; unit_distributions: Series; unit_nav: Series    # per $1, USD, dated; coerced and copied

    def events_by_day(self) -> list[tuple[Timestamp, float, float, float | None]]:   # (day, call, dist, mark)
        ...

    def align_history(self, timeline: Timeline) -> AlignedFundHistory:
        events, n = self.events_by_day(), timeline.n_observations
        calls, distributions, nav = np.zeros(n), np.zeros(n), np.zeros(n)
        j, running = 0, 0.0
        for t, observation in enumerate(timeline.dates):
            while j < len(events) and events[j][0] <= observation:        # pool onto the first observation on/after
                day, call, distribution, mark = events[j]
                running = mark if mark is not None else running + call - distribution
                if running < -1e-12: raise ValueError(f"Fund {self.name!r}: unit NAV would be {running:.6g} on {day.date()}")
                calls[t] += call; distributions[t] += distribution; j += 1
            nav[t] = max(running, 0.0)
        return AlignedFundHistory(calls, distributions, nav, timeline.first_observation_on_or_after(self.closing_date))


@dataclass(frozen=True, eq=False)
class Portfolio:
    base_currency: str                   # currency of the liquid index and of all reports
    liquid_levels: Series                # dated total-return levels, base currency; dates = the grid
    commitment_rates: DataFrame          # index = calendar year, columns = fund_type; contiguous years
    usd_rate: Series | None = None       # price of 1 USD in base currency, dated

    def __post_init__(self):
        ...                                                            # coerce, copy, validate levels and rates
        if self.base_currency == PRIVATE_CURRENCY:
            if self.usd_rate is not None: raise ValueError("usd_rate must be omitted when base_currency is USD")
        elif self.usd_rate is None:
            raise ValueError(f"usd_rate is required: base_currency {self.base_currency!r} is not USD")


# timeline.py
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Timeline:
    dates: DatetimeIndex                 # unique, increasing calendar dates
    def first_observation_on_or_after(self, day) -> int:      return int(self.dates.searchsorted(Timestamp(as_date(day)), side="left"))
    def first_observations_on_or_after(self, days) -> ndarray:   return self.dates.searchsorted(DatetimeIndex(days), side="left")
    def last_value_on_or_before(self, series) -> ndarray:   return series.sort_index().reindex(self.dates, method="ffill").to_numpy()


# state.py
@dataclass
class LiquidAccount:
    balance: float; returns: ndarray                            # the period's percent change; returns[0] = 0
    def apply_return(self, t):     pnl = self.balance * self.returns[t]; self.balance += pnl; return pnl
    def deposit(self, x):  self.balance += x
    def withdraw(self, x): short = max(0.0, x - self.balance); self.balance -= x; return short

@dataclass(frozen=True, eq=False)
class Commitment:
    fund: Fund; path: AlignedFundHistory; usd: float
    def calls_in_period(self, t):         return self.usd * self.path.unit_calls[t]
    def distributions_in_period(self, t): return self.usd * self.path.unit_distributions[t]
    def nav_at(self, t):           return self.usd * self.path.unit_nav[t] if t >= 0 else 0.0

@dataclass
class CommitmentBook:
    commitments: list[Commitment]
    def calls_in_period(self, t):         return math.fsum(c.calls_in_period(t) for c in self.commitments)
    def distributions_in_period(self, t): return math.fsum(c.distributions_in_period(t) for c in self.commitments)
    def nav_at(self, t):           return math.fsum(c.nav_at(t) for c in self.commitments)
    def commitments_closing_in(self, t):     return [c for c in self.commitments if c.closing_period == t]


# policy.py
class CommitmentPolicy(Protocol):
    def size_commitments(self, cohort: Sequence[Fund], balances: SizingBalances) -> Mapping[str, float]: ...

class AnnualRatePolicy:
    def __init__(self, rates, funds, weights=None, *, carry_forward=False, years=None):
        ...                                                            # validate rates cover years and types; weights per (year, type) group
        self.entitlements = {}                                         # fund name -> Entitlement(policy_year, fund_type, weight, draws)
        for fund_type in self.rates.columns:
            waiting = {}                                               # years with no fund of this type, waiting for one
            for year in self.rates.index:
                rate = self.rates.at[year, fund_type]
                cohort = groups.get((year, fund_type), [])
                for f in cohort:                                      # an explicit plan wins; else its own year plus what waited
                    draws = plans.get(f.name) or {year: 1.0, **waiting}
                    self.entitlements[f.name] = Entitlement(year, fund_type, w[f], draws)
                if cohort: waiting = {}
                elif self.carry_forward and rate > 0: waiting[year] = 1.0

    def size_commitments(self, cohort, balances):              # dollars are combined, never rates
        return {f.name: e.weight * (self.share(e.current_year_rate, balances.date, balances) * balances.liquid_only_usd
                                   + sum(self.share(rate, ye.date, balances) * ye.liquid_only_usd            # each carried year on its own year end
                                         for year, rate in e.carried_year_rates.items() for ye in [balances.year_end_on_or_before(year)]))
                for f in cohort for e in [self.entitlements[f.name]]}
    def share(self, schedule_rate, on, balances):              # the pacing schedule as a share of the liquid value
        return schedule_rate / (1 + self.expected_return) ** years_between(balances.first_commitment_date, on)
    def explain_commitment(self, fund_name, balances): ...       # expected_value, own_year_usd, other_years_usd, drawn_years, weight
    def drawn_years(self, fund_name, balances): ...               # a DrawnYear per year: rate, sizing_date, looks_ahead, dollars


# simulator.py
class Simulator:
    def __init__(self, portfolio, funds=(), policy=None, *, stop_on_shortfall=True, cash_tolerance=1e-9):
        self.timeline = Timeline(portfolio.liquid_levels.index)
        levels = portfolio.liquid_levels.to_numpy()
        self.returns = np.concatenate([[0.0], levels[1:] / levels[:-1] - 1.0])   # percent changes
        self.fx = self.timeline.last_value_on_or_before(portfolio.usd_rate) if portfolio.requires_fx_conversion else np.ones(self.timeline.n_observations)
        self.aligned_histories = {f.name: f.align_history(self.timeline) for f in funds}
        self.policy = policy or AnnualRatePolicy(portfolio.commitment_rates, funds, years=self.timeline.calendar_years)
        self.funds_beyond_horizon = tuple(f.name for f in funds if self.aligned_histories[f.name].closes_beyond_horizon)
        ...

    def run(self) -> SimulationResult:
        liquid = LiquidAccount(float(self.portfolio.liquid_levels.iloc[0]), self.returns)
        book, private_open, shortfall = CommitmentBook(), 0.0, None

        for t in range(self.timeline.n_observations):
            day, rate = self.timeline.observation_date(t), float(self.fx[t])          # rate: base currency per 1 USD

            liquid_open, private_nav_usd_open = liquid.balance, book.nav_at(t - 1)    # 1

            liquid_pnl = liquid.apply_return(t)                                       # 2

            distributions_existing = book.distributions_in_period(t) * rate           # 3
            liquid.deposit(distributions_existing)

            cohort = self._closings_by_period.get(t, [])                              # 4
            balances = self._sizing_balances(t, day, liquid.balance / rate, private_nav_usd_open)
            dollars_by_fund = self._size_commitments(cohort, balances)                # USD in, USD out
            for fund in cohort:
                book.add(Commitment(fund, self.aligned_histories[fund.name], dollars_by_fund[fund.name]))
                commitment_rows.append(self._commitment_row(t, day, fund, balances, dollars_by_fund[fund.name], rate))

            distributions_new = math.fsum(c.distributions_in_period(t)                # 5
                                          for c in book.commitments_closing_in(t)) * rate
            liquid.deposit(distributions_new)
            calls = book.calls_in_period(t) * rate
            unpaid = liquid.withdraw(calls)                                           # 0 when the account covered them

            private_close = book.nav_at(t) * rate                                     # 6
            period_rows.append({...})                                                 # balances, what moved them, what was committed
            fund_rows.extend(self._fund_rows(t, day, book, rate))
            private_open = private_close

            if unpaid > self.cash_tolerance and shortfall is None:
                shortfall = self._shortfall(t, day, book, calls, unpaid, rate)
                if self.stop_on_shortfall: break

        return SimulationResult(self.portfolio.base_currency, periods, funds, commitments,
                                shortfall, self.funds_beyond_horizon)

    def map_events_to_observations(self) -> DataFrame: ...     # every fund event and the observation it pooled onto

If performance ever matters, CommitmentBook is the one place to vectorise: stack the paths into (n, funds) arrays and keep usd as a vector. The interface does not change.

What the original got wrong, and where the design removes it

  • Hard-coded 20-year flows array — gone; everything is sized from the liquid dates.
  • Exposure bucket overwritten (vintage_exposures[0, …]) — gone; exposures are a pivot of recorded rows, never a mutable array.
  • Silent negative liquidity — LiquidAccount.withdraw reports the shortfall; the run stops by default.
  • Zero-commitment placeholder vintages every year — gone; a Commitment exists only once a fund closes.
  • Quadratic rescans of every vintage every year — each path is prepared once; the loop indexes arrays.
  • New vintages' same-period distributions lost — the original read a placeholder vintage's distributions before it had a commitment, so they were always zero. Here the cohort's closing-period distributions are banked in step 5, before calls: kept. They could never reach the sizing base anyway, which is the liquid-only value.

What the tests pin

  • Identities: all three accounting identities, opening = previous closing, and fund-table-to-period reconciliation, checked by a shared helper on every run that has one.
  • Worked examples: the sterling tables above to the penny including fx_translation; the same funds in USD give 66,000 / 48,400 / 1,208,350; carry-forward sizes 2027 and 2028 on their own year-end balances and gives 210,000 / 152,000 (pooled percentages would have given 225,000 / 180,000).
  • Timing: a closing between observations maps to the next one and keeps its calendar-year rate; a December closing observed in January uses December's rate; a first-date closing and same-day call are processed once; new-cohort distributions are banked after sizing; same-day gross flows both survive; an off-grid mark drives valuation P&L.
  • Observation frequency: intramonth flows pool onto the month end; monthly, quarterly and single-date grids agree; weekend flows move to the next pricing day; irregular grids; intramonth closings sized at the month end; map_events_to_observations.
  • Currency: GBP without a rate and USD with one both fail at Portfolio construction; sparse rates carry forward; commitments are fixed in USD at the closing rate.
  • Failure: a shortfall stops at the failed observation with the exact deficit and per-fund calls, or continues with a negative balance when allowed; a first-date shortfall leaves one recorded period; exactly zero cash is valid; an empty account does not shrink the next commitment; cash_tolerance absorbs rounding only.
  • Sizing: commitments are sized on the liquid-only value — the same liquid returns, initial value and schedule give the same commitments whatever the funds call or distribute; a pacing schedule is divided by the expected value, which is exactly 1 on the first commitment date and (1+X)^k on its anniversaries; carried years use their own year end's value and expected value; an expected return of 1 or more is rejected as a percentage typed as a number.
  • Validation: duplicate names, funds closing before inception, missing rate years or types, bad weights, invalid policy output, negative or non-finite values, intraday dates, history before the closing.
  • Benchmark: the worked example's comparison and PME figures by hand; value added is the same number three ways — total less liquid-only, index-compounded flows plus NAV, and fv_calls × (ks_pme − 1) — and adds up across funds and fund types, including through a shortfall; annualised_irr on known cases and where it is undefined.
  • Repeatability: permuting the fund list gives bit-identical tables; two runs match exactly; input objects are unchanged and copied from the caller's data.

This note describes the code in this folder: pmsim, built from scratch against these rules.
